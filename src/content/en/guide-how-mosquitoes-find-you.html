Female mosquitoes find people by stacking several cues: they pick up the carbon dioxide we breathe out, which makes them fly toward dark shapes, then follow body heat and skin odour at closer range. A 2014 study in Cell showed that sensing CO₂ switches on their attraction to heat and lactic acid, and a 2024 study in Nature found that Aedes aegypti can also sense infrared from skin up to about 70 cm away.

What is the first thing a mosquito notices?

Carbon dioxide. Every breath leaves a plume of CO₂ that drifts on the air, and host-seeking female mosquitoes react to it. In 2014, Conor McMeniman and colleagues in Leslie Vosshall's lab at Rockefeller University knocked out Gr3, a part of the CO₂ receptor in Aedes aegypti. Without it, mosquitoes no longer responded to heat and lactic acid the way normal mosquitoes did when CO₂ was present: CO₂ acts as a gate that turns other cues on. Yet the mutants could still find a live human in a semi-field test, because heat and odour together were enough. The authors concluded that no single cue is essential; the cues back each other up (McMeniman et al., Cell, 2014).

Can mosquitoes see you?

Yes, once they have smelled you. In a wind tunnel, Floris van Breugel and colleagues at Caltech and the University of Washington found that female mosquitoes ignored a dark, high-contrast object until they had flown through a CO₂ plume; after that, they explored it. A brief whiff was enough, even if the CO₂ was gone by the time they saw the object. Warmth was different: they preferred a warm object whether or not CO₂ was present. The study describes smell, vision and heat as independent modules that run in sequence (van Breugel et al., Current Biology, 2015).

How close does a mosquito need to be to feel your body heat?

Closer than for CO₂, but further than touching distance. In 2024, Avinash Chandel, Nicolas DeBeaubien and colleagues in Craig Montell's lab at UC Santa Barbara showed that Aedes aegypti detect thermal infrared with TRPA1, a heat-sensitive channel in neurons at the tip of the antenna. Infrared from a source at skin temperature (34 °C) roughly doubled host-seeking when combined with CO₂ and human odour, but infrared on its own had no effect. The effect reached about 70 cm (Chandel et al., Nature, 2024; UC Santa Barbara).

Why do mosquitoes bite some people more than others?

Skin chemistry is a big part of it. Maria Elena De Obaldia and colleagues tested the skin odour of volunteers against Aedes aegypti over several years. The most attractive people had much higher levels of carboxylic acids on their skin, and they stayed attractive across repeated tests. The study shows an association, not proof that the acids cause the preference (De Obaldia et al., Cell, 2022).

How does Mosquito Simulator model this?

We turned the science into rules you can feel with one thumb, and simplified it on purpose:

  • Breath plumes: every sleeper breathes out a warm, visible plume. Fly inside it and you lock onto the scent: an on-screen trail leads to skin and the next bites pay up to 50% more blood. That is CO₂ gating plus odour, compressed into one mechanic.
  • Heat sense: Culiseta and Deinocerites sense heat from further away, and Toxorhynchites further still. See the species page.
  • Long antennae, faster lock: Deinocerites males have very long antennae, so in the game Deinocerites locks onto a plume twice as fast. That one is a playful stretch: in reality the long antennae help males find pupae.
  • The other side: people find you by sight and sound, so light, noise and open eyes raise the room's alertness, and landing on a dark wall hides you.

What is invented: blood “tastes” (coffee, sugar, wine) are a game idea, not science.

What does this mean for avoiding bites?

Because the cues back each other up, blocking only one of them rarely works, as the 2014 study pointed out. The CDC recommends EPA-registered repellents with DEET, picaridin, IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus, para-menthane-diol or 2-undecanone, used as the label directs (CDC).
